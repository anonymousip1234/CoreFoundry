# Class 01 — Activity Design

**Bong Study Hub — Foundation Batch 2026 · Welcome to Computer Science**

Four required activities plus one optional activity, all drawn directly
from the Master Instructor Guide and Pen-Tablet Board Plan — no new
academic content. Activity 3 (Tea Activity) has its own dedicated,
much more detailed facilitator guide:
`03_Tea_Activity_Facilitator_Guide.md`. Treat this entry as its summary
card only.

---

## Activity 1 — "What is Computer Science?"

- **Objective:** Get students to discover, in their own words, that
  Computer Science is broader than coding — before the instructor states
  it formally.
- **Duration:** ~8–10 minutes (Guide §5.1–5.2 / Board Plan Drawing 1
  window, ~9–17 min mark).
- **Format:** Whole class, individual verbal answers, building into one
  shared live drawing (the CS map).
- **Instructions (overview):** Instructor asks "What is Computer Science?"
  and collects 3–5 answers without judgment. The words offered become the
  first satellite boxes drawn on the CS map. Instructor then adds 2–3
  fields nobody mentioned to prove the map is bigger than any single
  answer.
- **Instructor instructions:** Do not correct any answer as "wrong."
  Repeat each word back before drawing it. Explicitly say "you were all
  partly right" once every named field has a place on the map.
- **Student instructions:** "Call out what you think Computer Science is
  — any word, there's no wrong answer here."
- **Expected outcome:** Most students can restate, by the end, that coding
  is one part of Computer Science, not the whole of it.
- **Common failure modes:**
  - Silence on the first ask → instructor should wait the full 3-second
    rule before offering a prompt ("even one word is fine").
  - One student dominates all the answers → acknowledge, then explicitly
    invite others: "let's hear a word from someone who hasn't spoken yet."
- **Debrief questions:** Q2.2, Q2.3 from the Question Bank ("Is coding all
  of Computer Science, or one part of it?").
- **Concept taught:** Computer Science ≠ Coding; CS as a field with many
  areas (Board Plan Drawing 1).
- **Should students write anything:** Yes — copy the finished CS map
  (center circle + all boxes) into their notes once it's complete; see
  Board Plan §9 (Student Copying Strategy).

## Activity 2 — "Input → Process → Output"

- **Objective:** Move students from recognizing I-P-O in a given example
  (the calculator) to generating their own example independently.
- **Duration:** ~4–5 minutes, nested inside the ~10-minute I-P-O section
  (Guide §5.5 / Board Plan Drawing 2 window, ~33–39 min mark).
- **Format:** Individual thinking (30 seconds) → pair discussion (1–2
  minutes) → 2–3 pairs share with the whole class.
- **Instructions (overview):** After the calculator example is drawn and
  the face-unlock follow-up is discussed, instructor asks pairs to invent
  their own input/process/output example from daily life — not phone- or
  calculator-related, to force some independent thinking.
- **Instructor instructions:** Give a firm, visible time box (90 seconds)
  and count it down verbally at the halfway mark. Cold-call 2–3 pairs
  afterward rather than asking for volunteers only, so quieter pairs still
  get heard sometimes.
- **Student instructions:** "With the person next to you, come up with
  your own input → process → output example — something from your day
  that isn't the calculator or your phone unlocking."
- **Expected outcome:** Pairs can name a coherent input/process/output
  triple (e.g., ordering food: menu choice = input, kitchen preparing =
  process, meal delivered = output).
- **Common failure modes:**
  - A pair names an output with no clear input → instructor asks "what
    triggered that output?"
  - A pair over-thinks and picks something with a genuinely complex
    process → instructor reassures that the process box is allowed to
    stay a "mystery box" today, just like in the main example.
- **Debrief questions:** Q4.2, Q4.3 from the Question Bank.
- **Concept taught:** Input → Process → Output as a transferable mental
  model (Board Plan Drawing 2).
- **Should students write anything:** Yes — the 3-box skeleton with the
  calculator example is required copy; their own pair example is optional
  to write down (it's for practice, not for the permanent notes).

## Activity 3 — "Teach a Computer How to Make Tea"

- **Objective:** Make students *feel* the gap between a human instruction
  and a precise instruction, by having the instructor role-play a
  literal-minded computer.
- **Duration:** ~13–15 minutes (protected — Guide §4.1 marks this "No —
  core activity"; Board Plan does not assign this a numbered drawing
  because it is spoken interaction, not a diagram).
- **Format:** Whole class; one student (or a rotating few) gives
  instructions aloud while the rest of the class watches and reacts.
- **Instructions (overview):** See the full dedicated guide —
  `03_Tea_Activity_Facilitator_Guide.md` — for opening framing, rules,
  round structure, escalation, and debrief. Summary: instructor announces
  "Imagine I am a computer," a student gives tea-making instructions one
  step at a time, and the instructor does *exactly* and *only* what is
  literally said, asking clarifying questions at every gap ("where is the
  water?", "how much?", "what if there's no gas?").
- **Instructor instructions:** Stay in character as a literal computer.
  Never volunteer the missing detail — always ask for it. Never mock a
  student's instruction; treat every gap as expected and interesting, not
  as a mistake.
- **Student instructions:** "Give me instructions to make tea, exactly as
  if I were a computer that does nothing except exactly what you say, in
  exactly the order you say it."
- **Expected outcome:** The class visibly discovers, without being told,
  that human instructions are full of unstated assumptions — and that
  precision is what programming requires.
- **Common failure modes:**
  - The activity turns into laughing at the volunteer → instructor
    redirects energy toward "the computer," not the student (see the
    facilitator guide's "prevent chaos" section).
  - One student tries to instruction-hack around the literal-computer
    rules using overly technical language → instructor stays in-character
    and asks for the next level of clarification anyway.
- **Debrief questions:** Q8.1, Q8.2, Q8.3 from the Question Bank.
- **Concept taught:** "Programming requires us to turn human intentions
  into precise instructions" — the emotional and pedagogical centerpiece
  of the class.
- **Should students write anything:** No — per Board Plan §9, nothing
  from this activity is a reference diagram; it should be experienced,
  not transcribed.

## Activity 4 — "Build an Algorithm"

- **Objective:** Have students construct the "larger of two numbers"
  algorithm themselves, live, one step at a time — not receive it
  finished.
- **Duration:** ~10–12 minutes, nested inside the ~13-minute Drawing 3
  window (Guide §5.6 / Board Plan Drawing 3, ~43–56 min mark). This
  overlaps with, and is built through, the Problem → Logic → Algorithm →
  Program → Result chain.
- **Format:** Whole class, question-driven, with the algorithm written
  live on the board from student answers; optionally, give pairs 60
  seconds to draft their own steps before the whole-class version is
  built, for classes that want more individual thinking time first.
- **Instructions (overview):** Instructor poses A = 15, B = 21 and asks
  "how would you find the larger one?" Build the 4-step algorithm from
  student answers exactly as specified in the Guide and Board Plan
  Drawing 3, Stage 5.
- **Instructor instructions:** Never supply the next step yourself before
  asking for it. Accept a correct answer even if phrased differently than
  the Guide's wording ("check which is bigger" = "compare them"). Hold a
  visible pause after each question.
- **Student instructions:** "I'm going to give you two numbers. Tell me,
  step by step, exactly how you'd find the larger one — pretend you're
  explaining it to someone with zero assumptions."
- **Expected outcome:** The class arrives at (or close to): (1) take two
  numbers, (2) compare them, (3) if A > B, A is larger, (4) otherwise, B
  is larger.
- **Common failure modes:**
  - A student states the *answer* (21) instead of the *method* →
    instructor asks "21 is right — but what's the rule that gets us
    there every time?"
  - A student's steps only work for this specific A and B → instructor
    asks "would your steps still work if I gave you different numbers?"
- **Debrief questions:** Q6.1, Q6.4 from the Question Bank ("What if A and
  B are equal?").
- **Concept taught:** What an algorithm is; the Problem → Logic →
  Algorithm → Program → Result chain (Board Plan Drawing 3 — the single
  most important diagram of the class).
- **Should students write anything:** Yes — the five-box chain and the
  worked algorithm are the highest-priority copy item of the entire class
  (Board Plan §9).

## Optional Activity — ATM Withdrawal Algorithm

- **Objective:** Let students discover, in their own words, that a
  real-world algorithm often needs decisions ("if this, then that") —
  without teaching any branching syntax.
- **Duration:** ~5 minutes, only if time allows (Guide §5.8, "Optional
  second activity"). Cut first under any time pressure — see
  `05_Classroom_Interaction_Flow.md` for exactly when to skip it.
- **Format:** Small groups (3–4 students) or pairs, then 1–2 groups share
  aloud.
- **Instructions (overview):** Ask groups to write the steps for
  withdrawing cash from an ATM. Let them discover, unprompted where
  possible, that the algorithm needs to handle a wrong PIN, insufficient
  balance, or the machine being out of cash.
- **Instructor instructions:** Do not introduce the words "if/else" or
  "branching" formally. If a group surfaces a decision point unprompted,
  name it lightly: "notice your algorithm just grew a decision — you'll
  meet that properly when we start programming."
- **Student instructions:** "In your group, write the steps for
  withdrawing money from an ATM — what happens at each step, including
  anything that could go wrong."
- **Expected outcome:** At least one group's algorithm naturally includes
  a wrong-PIN or insufficient-balance branch, even if described in plain
  English rather than formal syntax.
- **Common failure modes:**
  - Groups only write the "happy path" (correct PIN, sufficient balance)
    and miss decisions entirely → instructor asks "what happens if the
    PIN is wrong?" to prompt discovery.
  - Groups try to write actual code → redirect to plain English steps.
- **Debrief questions:** "What decision did your algorithm need that the
  'larger of two numbers' algorithm didn't?"
- **Concept taught:** A light, ungraded preview of decision points inside
  algorithms — reinforces Algorithm (Section 6) without introducing new
  vocabulary.
- **Should students write anything:** Optional — this is a discovery
  activity, not a copy-required diagram; groups may jot notes for their
  own use only.
