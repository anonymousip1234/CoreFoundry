# Homework 2 — Instructor Grading Workflow

**Bong Study Hub — Foundation Batch 2026 · Class 02: How Machines Learn**

A simple, repeatable process for grading each submission. Budget
**~8–12 minutes per submission** — this assignment asks for real
explanation and reasoning across 10 questions, so it takes longer to
grade thoughtfully than Class 01's single-scenario assignment did.

---

## The 8-Step Process

### 1. Collect submissions
Accept any of the three formats (handwritten/photographed, typed
document, PDF) without penalty for format choice. If a submission is
missing questions entirely, note which ones before grading rather than
assuming they were skipped by mistake — you'll need this for Step 7.

### 2. Check completeness
Scan for all 10 required questions plus the optional reflection (not
graded, but worth a glance). Missing answers should be scored 0 for
that question rather than guessed at — do not infer an answer the
student didn't write.

### 3. Grade against the rubric
Work through `Class_02_Homework_Rubric.md` question by question, not
submission by submission across all questions at once — grading the
same question across several submissions in a row (batch-by-question)
keeps your internal standard more consistent than jumping between all
10 questions for one student before moving to the next (see the
Batching Strategy below).

### 4. Look for conceptual understanding
For every answer, ask: *does this show the student understands the
idea, even if the wording is different from class?* Re-read the "What
This Rubric Does NOT Grade" section before your first submission of
each batch — vocabulary mismatches are not the same as conceptual
errors, and only the latter should cost points.

### 5. Add one positive comment
Pick (or adapt) one comment from `Class_02_Homework_Feedback_Comments.md`
that names something the student did well, specifically — not a
generic "good job."

### 6. Add one improvement suggestion
Pick (or adapt) one constructive comment that points at the single
biggest gap — not every gap. Two to three sentences total feedback is
enough; a first-year student benefits more from one clear thing to
revisit than a list of every minor issue.

### 7. Record score
Log the score (out of 20) and your comments in your gradebook or
tracking sheet. Keep a copy of particularly strong or particularly
struggling submissions as reference points for calibrating later
batches.

### 8. Identify students needing clarification
Flag a student for a quick follow-up (in the next class, or a short
1:1 message) if **any** of the following apply:
- Score is 10/20 or below.
- Q4 (Core Pipeline) or Q5 (Traditional vs. ML) scores 0 — these are
  the two questions closest to the class's core concept, and a miss
  here suggests the central idea didn't land.
- Q7 (Prediction ≠ Certainty) is answered "Yes" (the student thinks a
  wrong prediction means the system wasn't really using Machine
  Learning) — this is the single most important misconception to
  catch and correct early.
- The student submitted nothing, or submitted something unrelated to
  the assignment.

Do not flag a student solely for imperfect vocabulary, messy
handwriting, or skipping the optional reflection — none of those
indicate a conceptual gap in this assignment's actual learning
objectives.

---

## Batching Strategy (Grade by Question, Not by Student)

For this assignment, grade **one question at a time across the whole
batch** rather than one student's full submission at a time:

1. Read all students' Q1 answers, score them, move to Q2, and so on
   through Q10.
2. This keeps your mental model of "what full credit looks like" fixed
   for each question, instead of drifting as you move through 10
   different questions per student.
3. It also makes Step 4's "watch for" patterns (see the rubric's
   "Common misconception" notes) much easier to spot — if five
   students all answer Q7 with "Yes," that's an instant, visible
   signal (see Step 8, below).

If the cohort grows beyond what one pass of this kind comfortably
allows in one sitting, split the batch by question **and** by group
size (e.g., grade Q1–Q5 for all students in one sitting, Q6–Q10 in a
second) rather than shortening your attention per submission — a
rushed read of a reasoning-heavy answer is where inconsistent grading
creeps in.

---

## Calibration Tip

Before grading a full batch, grade 3–5 submissions' Q4 and Q7 answers
first (the two most conceptually central questions) and compare your
scores against each other. If your scores for similar-quality answers
drift by more than ~1 point across that sample, re-read the rubric's
descriptors for that question once more before continuing —
consistency matters more than speed here.

---

## After Grading

- Note any pattern across the batch (e.g., "many students answered Q7
  with 'Yes'," or "several students listed 'patterns' as a fifth
  pipeline stage in Q4") — this is exactly the kind of signal the
  Master Instructor Guide's Instructor Reflection Checklist and
  Section 35 (Post-Class Reflection Prompts) are built to capture.
  **Feed common misconceptions into how you open the next class** —
  a quick "last time, a few of you said X — let's clear that up"
  is more effective than a written correction alone.
- Return feedback within a timeframe students can still act on before
  related material builds further — faster feedback matters more than
  exhaustive feedback for a conceptual, formative assignment like this
  one.

This workflow does not require or assume any automated/AI grading
tool — everything above is designed to be done by a human instructor,
by hand, in the time budgeted.
