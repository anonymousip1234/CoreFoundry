# Class 02 Assessment — Grading Workflow

**Bong Study Hub — Foundation Batch 2026 · Class 02: How Machines Learn**

A simple, repeatable process for grading each submission. Budget
**~7–10 minutes per submission** — slightly less than the Homework's
8–12 minutes, since this assessment has 7 focused reasoning questions
rather than 10.

---

## The 9-Step Process

### 1. Collect submissions
Accept any reasonable format (handwritten/photographed, typed
document, PDF) without penalty for format choice.

### 2. Check completeness
Scan for all 7 required questions plus the optional reflection (not
graded, but worth a glance). A missing answer scores 0 for that
question — do not infer an answer the student didn't write.

### 3. Grade against the rubric
Work through `Class_02_Assessment_Rubric.md` **one question at a time
across the whole batch**, not one student's full submission at a
time — this keeps your sense of "what full credit looks like" fixed
per question instead of drifting across seven different questions per
student. (See the Batching Strategy below.)

### 4. Look for conceptual understanding
For every answer, ask: *does this show the student can reason with
the idea in a scenario they've never seen, or are they reciting
something memorized that doesn't quite fit?* Re-read the rubric's
"What This Rubric Does NOT Grade" section before your first submission
of each batch.

### 5. Identify misconceptions
As you grade, actively watch for the rubric's named misconception
patterns — especially:
- **Question 5 answered "Yes"** (prediction treated as certainty) —
  the single highest-priority misconception on this assessment.
- **Question 3's Model/Prediction swap.**
- **Question 1 framed as an effort/time problem rather than a
  variety/scale problem.**
- **Question 7 answered with rule-based reasoning** instead of a
  learning-based approach.

### 6. Record score
Log the score (out of 20) in your gradebook or tracking sheet. Keep a
copy of particularly strong or particularly struggling submissions as
calibration references for later batches.

### 7. Give concise feedback
Unlike the Homework, do not draw from a large comment bank — write
**one short, specific note** (1–2 sentences) naming the single
highest-value thing to reinforce or revisit. Match this style:

> "You've got the central idea. Revisit the difference between a
> Model and a Prediction — the model is what the learning process
> builds, while the prediction is what it produces for a new case."

> "Good reasoning about the unfamiliar example. One thing to
> strengthen: connect the system's difficulty back to what it
> actually saw during learning."

Lead with what the student did well whenever the score allows it
honestly — never open a comment with the gap. Never use language that
could feel humiliating; a wrong answer here is a normal, expected part
of learning to transfer a new mental model.

### 8. Flag students needing follow-up
Flag a student for a quick follow-up (in the next class, or a short
1:1 message) if **any** of the following apply:
- Score is 10/20 or below.
- Question 3 (Pipeline Reasoning) scores 0–1 — this is the highest-
  weight question and closest to the class's central idea.
- Question 5 is answered "Yes" (prediction treated as certainty).
- The student reverts to explicit rule-based reasoning in **two or
  more** questions (a pattern, not an isolated slip).
- The student appears unable to apply the mental model to the
  unfamiliar Question 6 or 7 scenarios at all (score of 0 on both).

**Do not flag a student merely because they use different
terminology** from class, or because of handwriting, English fluency,
or a low score on a single non-central question in isolation.

### 9. Record cohort-level misconception patterns
Note any pattern across the whole batch (e.g., "many students answered
Question 5 with 'Yes'," or "several students swapped Model and
Prediction in Question 3"). Feed this into how you open the next
class or a brief revisit — this is exactly the kind of signal the
Master Instructor Guide's reflection prompts and the Homework grading
workflow are both built to capture, now extended to the assessment.

---

## Calibration Procedure

Before grading the full batch:

1. Grade **3–5 sample submissions** first.
2. Pay particular attention to **Question 3 (Pipeline Reasoning)** and
   **Question 5 (Prediction vs. Certainty)** — these are the two
   questions where scoring drift is most likely and most consequential.
3. Compare your scores for similar-quality answers on these two
   questions across your sample.
4. If your scores drift by more than ~1 point for comparable answers,
   re-read the rubric's descriptors for that question once more before
   continuing with the full batch.

---

## Batching Strategy (Grade by Question, Not by Student)

Grade **one question at a time across the whole cohort**, in this
order of priority if time is limited:

1. Question 3 (Pipeline Reasoning) — highest point value, most central
   concept.
2. Question 5 (Prediction vs. Certainty) — highest-priority
   misconception check.
3. Questions 1, 2, 4, 6, 7 in any convenient order.

If the cohort grows large enough that grading all seven questions for
everyone in one sitting isn't practical, split by question groups
(e.g., Q1–Q4 in one sitting, Q5–Q7 in a second) rather than rushing
through full submissions — a hurried read of a reasoning-heavy answer
is where inconsistent grading creeps in.

---

## After Grading

- Return feedback within a timeframe students can still act on before
  related material builds further.
- Use the cohort-level misconception notes from Step 9 to shape a
  brief opening recap in the next class — a two-minute "a few of you
  said X, let's clear that up" is more effective than written
  correction alone.
- This workflow does not require or assume any automated/AI grading
  tool — everything above is designed to be done by a human
  instructor, by hand, in the time budgeted.
