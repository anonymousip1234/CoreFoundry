## What This Assignment Is About

In class, we asked how a computer can represent information as
different as a number, a word, a photograph, or a song — using nothing
but combinations of two-state units called **bits**. We saw that one
bit gives two possibilities, that more bits give more combinations,
and that a bit pattern only means something once we agree on how to
interpret it.

This assignment asks you to **reason with these ideas, not recite
them**. There is no binary arithmetic, no conversion between binary and
decimal, and no single "correct sentence" to memorize — if your
reasoning makes sense, your answer is good, even if it doesn't use the
exact words from class.

## Answers

**Q1 — Information Needs Representation (2 marks)**
A computer cannot "just understand" a real-world thing (a number, a
photo, a sentence) the way a person does — it needs the information
turned into some stored form it can actually hold onto and work with.
(1 mark for stating a computer can't directly "understand" real-world
things; 1 mark for connecting this to needing a stored/held form.)

**Q2 — Two-State Systems (2 marks)**
A system with two clearly, reliably distinguishable states (like a
light switch) is simple to build and combine reliably, over and over,
at massive scale — you never get confused about which state it's in.
Accept any answer that captures "reliable/easy to tell apart" as the
reason. (No credit for answers describing voltage/electronics.)

**Q3 — What Is a Bit? (2 marks)**
A bit represents one of two possible states, conventionally written as
0 or 1. Full credit for any phrasing capturing "one of two states";
no credit if the answer treats a bit as "a special kind of number" or
skips the two-state idea entirely.

**Q4 — One Bit (2 marks)**
Two. One bit can only ever be one of two states, so it can only signal
one of two possible messages (e.g., a single yes/no answer).

**Q5 — Different Kinds of Information (2 marks)**
Any three of: text, images/photographs, sound/audio, video (numbers
don't count toward the three, since the question asks for kinds
besides numbers). Full credit for any three reasonable kinds — no
technical encoding explanation required or expected.

**Q6 — Two Bits (3 marks)**
`00, 01, 10, 11` — four combinations total. Accept any reasoning for
"how you know you found them all" that shows systematic listing (e.g.,
"I paired each first-bit value with both possible second-bit values").
(1 mark for the correct list, 1 mark for stating four, 1 mark for a
sound explanation of completeness.)

**Q7 — Predicting Growth (3 marks)**
The answer is 16. Full credit for reasoning such as "each extra bit
doubles the previous count, and 8 doubled is 16" — no formula (2ⁿ)
required or expected. Partial credit (1–2 marks) for a correct doubling
description that arrives at the wrong final number due to an
arithmetic slip.

**Q8 — Representation ≠ Information (3 marks)**
"80% full" is a recorded representation of the crowded canteen, not the
canteen itself. A bit pattern is exactly the same kind of thing — a
representation of information, not the information itself, and
definitely not the real-world thing the information is about. Full
credit requires the student to explicitly connect both examples as the
same *kind* of relationship (representation vs. the real thing), not
just describe each separately.

**Q9 — Where Does Meaning Come From? (3 marks)**
No — the meanings do not come from the bits themselves. The same four
patterns (`00/01/10/11`) could just as easily have been assigned
completely different meanings; the meaning comes from the system/
agreement someone designed, not from anything inherent in the bits.
Full credit requires a clear "no, because..." with a reasoning that
meaning is assigned/agreed upon, not intrinsic.

**Q10 — Create Your Own Representation (3 marks)**
Accept any coherent 4-way mapping using `00/01/10/11`. Check for: (1)
all four bit patterns are used exactly once, (2) the four meanings are
genuinely distinct, (3) the explanation shows the student understands
the mapping is a choice they made, not something forced by the bits.

**Q11 — Connecting Class 03 and Class 04 (2 marks)**
Blanks = **REPRESENTATION** then **BITS** (in that order). The
one-sentence explanation should convey that Class 04 added
`REPRESENTATION → BITS` between Class 03's `DATA` and the idea of a
computer actually processing it — i.e., explaining *how* data ends up
inside a computer, not changing anything about Class 03's chain itself.

**Q12 — Synthesis (3 marks)**
No fixed wording expected. Look for the core idea: a computer doesn't
need different hardware for every kind of information — the same
building block (bits) can be combined differently to represent very
different things, which makes one general-purpose machine capable of
handling numbers, text, images, sound, and video alike. Award full
marks for any answer that captures "one building block, many kinds of
information, that's what makes it powerful/efficient/flexible."
